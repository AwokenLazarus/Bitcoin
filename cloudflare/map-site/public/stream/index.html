Whitepaper · XBTLightning Stream: pay as it arrives

The people who run Lightning nodes should be able to connect any service they want to offer to the network (a file or media server, AI compute, storage, software of their own) and be paid for it piece by piece, by anyone. Sometimes that is one buyer and one seller, as with a streamed film. Sometimes it is many sellers at once, as in a torrent swarm, or people lending their hardware for AI.

Lightning Stream is an upgrade to Lightning built for that: a way for people and AI agents to pay for a service while it is being delivered, an answer token by token, a file chunk by chunk, an API call by call. A few small payments are locked ahead of the service, each request releases one, and the ones not used come back. For pieces too small to be worth a payment of their own, a fair roll decides which pieces are paid, and the average comes out at the exact price.

Draft 5 · 12 October 2026 · Lightning Stream (source). The direction on this page is decided and in design. Stream tickets have been tested in a lab on a test network; they are not built into the wallet yet. The lottery for amounts smaller than a ticket has been designed and measured in the same kind of lab; it is not built either, and one of its two forms waits for an outside review. Nothing here runs on XBT mainnet. An outside review of the earlier design found serious issues; see The earlier design and Status.

What the name means. Lightning Stream is built on Lightning, not beside it. It adds the stream ticket to ordinary XBT Lightning channels, with a lottery for amounts smaller than a ticket, and uses Lightning's own routing, liquidity and nodes for everything else. It is not a separate channel system. An earlier draft of this paper described one, with its own channel on the chain and its own hub; what became of it is in The earlier design.

Lightning made payments fast. Stream tickets make them continuous.

1The problem

More and more of what we buy arrives over time: the tokens of a model's answer, the minutes of a stream, the pages of a dataset, the thousandth call to an API. Machines are now the buyers as often as people, and the prices are tiny: fractions of a cent, thousands of times an hour.

Most ways to pay for that ask someone to trust someone. Pay up front and you trust the seller to deliver. Pay afterwards and the seller trusts you. A subscription or an API key trusts a company with your card. An on-chain payment for each piece is honest but far too slow and too costly.

Lightning already moves small payments between strangers in a moment, with nobody holding the money in the middle. What it lacks is a way to pay for something that arrives in thousands of pieces without making every piece wait for its payment. In our lab one Lightning payment per piece took 110 ms on a direct channel and 210 ms through one routing node (median), and each payment through a routing node paid that node's base fee. Lightning Stream adds the missing part, and leaves the rest of Lightning as it is.

2The idea

Lock a few small Lightning payments before the service starts. Release one with each request. Take back the ones you did not use.

That is the whole addition. It needs no change to the Lightning node software: in our lab it ran through a routing node, on the node software an XBT Lightning fork ships today. Everything else in this paper is what a ticket gives, what it costs, and how software that should never be trusted with a key can use it.

3How a ticket works

An ordinary Lightning payment is locked to a hash, and whoever shows the secret behind the hash collects it. Normally the seller picks that secret, so the money is the seller's the moment it arrives. A ticket turns that round: the buyer picks the secret.

Terms
The seller answers the first request with its price and terms: its Lightning node, the size of a ticket, how many tickets it will hold for one buyer, and how long a ticket lives.
Lock
The wallet makes one secret for each ticket and sends the seller only the hashes. For each hash the seller's node issues a hold invoice: an invoice that can be paid but not collected until its secret is shown. The wallet checks each invoice (who is paid, how much, that it is an XBT invoice, and that it expires no later than the owner allows) and pays it through the owner's Lightning node.
Pay
Each request carries the next secret. The seller hashes it, confirms that the matching ticket is locked, serves the request, and collects the ticket. Inside one ticket, pieces are counted in millisatoshis against a running total that the seller signs in its receipt.
Refill
When few unreleased tickets are left, the wallet locks more in the background. No request waits for a payment.
Stop
The wallet tells the seller it is done, and the seller cancels the unreleased tickets, which frees the buyer's money at once. If the seller does not, its node cancels each ticket 18 blocks before the ticket expires, or the ticket times out along the route.

Letting the buyer choose the secret was proposed for Lightning in 2019 under the name "stuckless payments". Tickets get the same effect from hold invoices, which Lightning nodes already have. It has a price: the secret no longer proves payment, because the buyer always knew it. The proof is the seller's signed receipt, together with the settled payment on the buyer's own node.

By default the buyer goes first: it releases a ticket with the request, so the seller risks nothing and the buyer risks one ticket. For content that can be checked piece by piece the order turns round: the buyer checks the piece, then releases the ticket, and it is the seller who risks one piece (see Paying only for what checks out). The seller's terms say which order applies.

The default settings are 1,000 sat tickets, 8 locked at a time, a life of about 40 blocks, and no ticket under 200 sat when the payment is routed. The seller sets them and the owner's policy caps them. These steps are the design. The lock, release and cancel steps were run in our lab by scripts driving three Lightning nodes; the wallet and seller software that will do them are not built yet.

4What it gives and what it costs

The measurements in this section come from a three-node regtest lab with one hop: a buyer, one routing node and a seller. Numbers has the full table and what was not measured.

What it gives

No extra round trip per request
The secret rides in the request. The seller's check took 0.93 ms (median) and collecting the ticket 3.5 ms (measured on a three-node regtest lab, one hop).
Pieces metered to the millisatoshi
Pieces are counted in millisatoshis, while money moves a ticket at a time: 1,000 sat by default, and never less than 1 sat. The part of the last ticket that was not used is trusted to the seller. For pieces worth less than a ticket there is a second way to pay: the lottery.
A bounded loss
The buyer can lose at most one ticket. A ticket that was not released is never paid.
Lightning's reach
A buyer can pay any seller a Lightning route reaches. The routing, the liquidity and the nodes are Lightning's own. Lightning Stream runs no network and no hub.
The owner's policy
The agent still pays under the owner's signed policy and still holds no key. In the lab the node permissions the wallet's Lightning payer already uses were enough to pay tickets. The seller's software needed only the permissions to read and write invoices, and its node refused it when it tried to send coins.

What it costs

Unused tickets are locked until cancelled or expired
A buyer that stops gets its unused tickets back at once only if the seller cancels them. Otherwise it waits: 25 blocks for 40-block tickets when the seller did nothing, 51 blocks through one routing node when the seller vanished (measured on a three-node regtest lab, one hop). At XBT's block spacing of about 7 minutes that is about 3 hours and about 6 hours.
The seller runs a Lightning node
A seller needs a Lightning node with inbound capacity, online while tickets are locked. This is a real demand, and it is also the aim of the design: every seller is one more Lightning node on XBT, and more nodes is what the network needs. A buyer needs a Lightning node with a channel, which the wallet's Lightning payer already asks for.
Tickets are held payments
While a ticket is locked it occupies capacity, and one of a channel's 483 payment slots, on every node along the route. A routing node earns a fee only when a ticket is collected, and nothing when it is cancelled. XBT's Lightning network today is two implementations and about forty channels, and there this is tolerable. On a large network it is the behaviour that defences against channel jamming are designed to stop. So tickets must stay few, small and short-lived, and the defaults are modest for that reason.
A routing fee per ticket
At the forks' default policy a routing node charges 1 sat, plus one part per million, for each ticket that is collected: 0.1% of a 1,000 sat ticket per hop. With 10 tickets locked and 5 collected, the buyer paid 5,005 sat and the seller received 5,000 (measured on a three-node regtest lab, one hop). What nodes on mainnet charge is not known to us.
The seller must collect on use and stay online
A ticket not collected 18 blocks before its expiry is cancelled by the seller's own node, and a seller offline when locked tickets expire had its channel force-closed (measured on a three-node regtest lab, one hop).
Hold invoices, BOLT 11 only
Tickets need a node with hold invoices. The fork we tested has them for BOLT 11 invoices and not for BOLT 12 offers. We have not tested the other XBT Lightning implementation, the Core Lightning fork, as a seller.
What others can see
No transaction on the chain ties a buyer to a seller, and the seller does not learn the buyer's node. But the nodes along the route see a run of equal payments to one seller and can see when each is released, and every node on a ticket's route sees the same hash.

5Smaller than a ticket: the lottery

Some things are worth less than the smallest payment worth sending. One piece of a shared file may be worth a tenth of a sat. Lightning carries nothing below 1 sat, and at the forks' default policy a routing node charges about 1 sat to carry any payment at all. A ticket does not help a seller who will earn less than one ticket from a buyer, and in a swarm of forty sellers that is most of them.

The answer is old and simple. Do not pay for every piece. Roll a fair die for every piece instead. Most rolls pay nothing. Now and then a roll wins, and a win pays an amount large enough to be worth sending. The odds are set so that the average is the price. With pieces at a tenth of a sat and a win of 64 sat, the odds are 1 in 640: over 640 pieces the buyer pays 64 sat on average, which is a tenth of a sat for each. Most pieces move no money at all.

Why neither side can steer the roll

Each roll is made from two halves. The seller fixes its half of every roll before the session starts and shows only a fingerprint of them, so it cannot change one later. The buyer adds its half only after the piece has arrived, and for content it can check, only after the check passed. The two halves are hashed together and the result decides. When each side fixed its half it could not know the other's, and afterwards both can work out the result for themselves. Because the buyer's half comes after the piece, a seller cannot serve only the pieces that win. This argument treats the hash function, SHA-256, as a fair source of randomness. It is our own, and nobody outside the project has reviewed it.

What it feels like

Uneven in the short run, exact in the long run. A miner knows the feeling: no block for a while, then one, and over a year the income matches the work. A seller's income from rolls evens out with the number of wins, and a seller chooses how often it wins by choosing the size of a win: wins in a day are the day's earnings divided by the win size. A buyer sees the same from the other side. One cheap download may cost a third more or a third less than its price, and over many downloads the average is the price. The costs, stated plainly has the figures.

Two forms

The buyer pays after a win
Nothing is locked ahead. When a roll wins, the seller sends an ordinary Lightning invoice with the proof of the win, and the wallet checks the win itself and pays. This is simple, and it needs nothing but ordinary invoices. Its weakness is plain: a buyer can see a win and not pay. The seller notices within seconds and stops serving, and by then the cheat has had about one win's worth of service for free: 64 sat on average at that size, and about three times that one time in twenty (simulated). Where a seller would earn less than one win from a buyer in any case, a buyer that never pays gets everything for free. So this form is a cheap and exact way for honest buyers to pay tiny amounts, with a small, bounded loss to each cheat. It is not enforcement.
A win that pays itself
The buyer first sets money aside in a locked box at the seller: an ordinary stream ticket, locked over Lightning, which only its key opens. Each roll then gives the seller a small chance of drawing the key, in a way that hides from the buyer whether it did. A seller that draws the key opens the box itself. The buyer cannot refuse to pay a win, because it learns of the win only when the money moves. After every roll, won or lost, the seller can check that the key really was there to be drawn. In our tests a buyer that offered rolls with no key in them was caught on half of those rolls, so a cheat gets about two pieces for free in place of a whole win's worth.

Where the two forms stand. Both are designed, and both were measured in a regtest lab, by scripts and prototypes. Neither is built into the wallet or into any seller's software. The first will be built first. The second rests on an argument of our own, that a cheating buyer fails the seller's check on at least half of its rolls, which no cryptographer has reviewed; it will not be built until one has. On today's Lightning it also needs one proof for each locked payment, to show the seller that the box's lock and the rolls use the same key. We measured one such proof at a quarter of a second to make, and it needs a setup step that must not be trusted to one party.

In the lab a roll of the first form cost about two millionths of a second for both sides together. A win was paid about a quarter of a second after it was rolled, through one routing node, and each win paid that node 1 sat, which is 1.6% of a 64 sat win. A roll of the second form cost 16 millionths of a second for each side and 200 to 600 bytes; in 20 runs out of 20 the seller collected its win alone, and the buyer's node showed the payment complete 13 ms after the win. Numbers has the rest.

6How it comes together

This is one session as it is designed, from the first request to the last, with a seller the buyer stays with and pieces smaller than a ticket. None of it is built yet.

Terms
A node runner puts a gateway in front of a service it already runs and sets a price for a piece. The gateway answers a first request with its terms: the price of a piece, the size of a win, and so the odds.
Limits
The wallet checks those terms against the owner's limits: the largest win, the highest odds, what any one seller may be paid, and the budget for the task. Terms outside the limits are refused before anything is paid.
Set aside
The wallet locks one payment with the seller over Lightning. Nothing has been spent. It is money set aside.
Roll
Each request carries the buyer's half of a roll. The roll rides on the request, so the service does not wait for it.
Win
When a roll wins, the seller collects the payment that was set aside. The wallet sees the money move on its own node, and books what its node paid, never a number the seller sends.
Top up
The wallet sets the next payment aside in the background, and the session goes on.
Stop
When the buyer stops, the seller cancels the payment that was set aside, and what was not won comes back, as an unused ticket does.

Which way of paying is used follows from the size of a piece and from how long the seller stays.

CaseHow it is paid
A piece worth a ticket or moreIt releases a ticket.
Anything smaller, from a seller the buyer stays withA roll. The win is sized so that the session expects about sixteen wins, and is never below 64 sat when the payment passes through a routing node.
A seller touched only briefly, as most sellers in a swarm areA roll, paid after a win, with nothing set aside.
A single call from a buyer with no sessionOne ordinary Lightning payment.

7Many sellers at once

Nothing in a ticket ties a buyer to one seller: each ticket is its own Lightning payment. In a five-node lab (a buyer, one routing node, and three seller nodes standing in for up to 200 sellers) one buyer with one channel held tickets with 200 sellers at the same time: two each, 400 tickets, locked in 11 seconds. One request that paid all 200 in the same instant was collected by every seller within 0.8 s. A seller that had just appeared could be paid a quarter of a second later through one routing node, and a tenth of a second later over a direct channel. With the lottery's pay-after-win form, 200 sellers that all won in the same instant had all been paid 4.6 s later, with nothing held for any of them beforehand.

Three limits remain, and each has an answer.

A ticket cannot be re-aimed
Once a Lightning payment is locked, its route and its receiver are sealed. Money cannot be locked first and pointed at a seller later, and a locked ticket cannot be moved to another seller. What can be done is to lock a new one, which is the quarter second above. The answer is not to wait for it: for content the buyer can check, the seller serves the first piece while the ticket locks, and paying after a win needs no lock at all.
Tickets are too coarse for small sellers
Through a routing node each collected payment costs about 1 sat, so paying one seller less than about 100 sat wastes more than 1%, and below 1 sat nothing can be paid. In a swarm most sellers earn far less than that. The answer is the lottery.
Held tickets are a shared, limited resource
A channel carries 483 held payments across all sellers: 60 sellers at 8 tickets each, 241 at 2. A seller that leaves without cancelling keeps its tickets locked for hours, and in a swarm sellers leave all the time. The answer is to hold little: one or two small tickets for each seller when there are many, one where a win pays itself, and none where the buyer pays after a win.

An owner cannot list sellers it has never heard of. In place of a list of names it approves a kind of spending: a budget for the task, and a small cap on what any one unknown seller can be paid. That part of the policy is designed, not built.

8The costs, stated plainly

The spreads below are simulations. "Nine days in ten" means the middle nine tenths of the days simulated.

Uneven income for a seller
A seller that earns about 4,000 sat a day in pieces of a tenth of a sat, with wins of 64 sat, is paid between 80% and 120% of what it earned nine days in ten, and between 96% and 104% over a month. A seller that earns 1,000 sat a day sees 58% to 141% on a day and 92% to 108% over a month. A seller that earns 100 sat a day gets no win at all one day in five.
The seller chooses the trade
A smaller win means steadier income and a larger share lost to routing fees, because each win costs about 1 sat for each routing node it passes: 6.25% of a 16 sat win, 1.6% of 64, 0.4% of 250, 0.1% of 1,000. A node that runs several services gets steadier income for nothing, because all their wins land on one node: twenty services that each earn 1,000 sat a day bring that node within 9% of its earnings on a day.
Uneven cost for a buyer
A download priced at 1,638 sat (4 GiB at a tenth of a sat a piece, wins of 64 sat) cost between 1,152 and 2,176 sat nine times in ten. For it not to stall on an unlucky run more than once in a thousand, the owner must allow 2,795 sat, fees included. The budget is a ceiling. The average stays at the price.
The seller goes first when the buyer pays after a win
By about one win's worth for each new buyer, and by everything where a seller earns less than one win from a buyer. Nothing on today's Lightning can force a buyer to pay a seller it touched only briefly.
What routing nodes carry
Tickets are held payments, and a win that pays itself needs one held for each seller. A routing node is paid nothing for one that is cancelled. If a seller vanishes with tickets locked, the routing node that opened the channel to it bears a forced close: with 100 tickets of 1,000 sat locked that cost about 29,000 sat in all at 1 sat/vB, which is 29% of the tickets' value, and with 3 tickets 1,530 sat (measured on regtest). A locked payment below a channel's dust limit, 354 sat in our lab, cannot be claimed on the chain at all. Paying after a win holds nothing, and pays the routing node for every payment it carries.
Pooling does not work here
Miners even out their income in pools. That does not carry over, because a mining share is proof of work that anyone can check, and a piece served proves nothing to anyone who was not there: a seller and a friendly buyer could invent pieces and draw on the pool for free.
The deeper help is in Lightning itself
Changes to Lightning would do more than anything we can add on top of it. Point locks first: with them the self-paying win would no longer need its proof. Then a fee for held payments, which would pay routing nodes for what tickets ask of them. Neither exists on the XBT Lightning forks. They are other people's software. We can ask for them, and we cannot promise them.

9Paying over HTTP

The web has had a status code for this since 1997: 402 Payment Required. The x402 standard gives it a shape: the server answers with a price and terms, and the client retries with a payment in a header. Lightning Stream uses that shape. The wire and the receipts are built, for the earlier channel. For tickets the seller's 402 gains one more entry in its list of accepted payments, with its own network id, and the retry carries a ticket's secret where it used to carry a signed channel state. That entry is designed, not built. The lottery will need an entry of its own, whose terms carry the size of a win and the odds and whose requests carry the roll; it is not specified yet. Upstream x402 clients will not know it, so only Lightning Stream clients will be able to pay with tickets.

Each receipt is signed by the seller and bound to the hash of the request it paid for. With tickets the receipt matters more than before, because it is the only proof of payment the buyer can show: the secret proves nothing. A client that does not check its receipts has no such proof. An answer lost on the way is paid for, since the secret was spent when it left the wallet, and a refusal (a 402) is not signed.

For a single call from a buyer with no session, an ordinary Lightning payment is the right tool, and a seller's 402 can offer that beside tickets.

On the wire this is x402 v2. Networks are named in CAIP-2 form. Because XBT and the SHA-256 chain share a genesis block, XBT mainnet is keyed to its fork block, 961,640, instead of genesis: bip122:0000000000000050c1e5f69672f45929. XBT is Bitcoin on BLAKE2b proof of work, and up to that block it has the same history as the SHA-256 chain, so its Lightning invoices look alike on both. What marks an XBT Lightning node, invoice or offer is feature bit 512. The wallet checks it on everything it pays, and it pays only through channels whose funding it can prove exists on XBT alone.

10Paying only for what checks out

Paying per piece only helps if a bad piece can be caught. For content that exists before it is sold, such as a file, a dataset or a recorded stream, the seller first signs a manifest: the Merkle root of every chunk it promises to send. Then the stream begins.

This is where the order of a ticket turns round: the buyer checks a chunk first and releases its ticket second. The last chunk can be sold the opposite way, as an ordinary Lightning payment to a hash the seller chose, so that its key arrives only with the payment. Fair exchange is built and tested on the earlier channel. On tickets it is designed, and simpler than it was, but not built.

This only works for fixed content. Nobody can commit to the Merkle root of a model's answer before the answer exists. For generated output, paying per piece still limits what the buyer can lose to one ticket, but it cannot prove that a piece is any good.

11A wallet for agents

An AI model should be able to spend, but never to hold a key: a model can be tricked by what it reads. So the agent never signs. It asks a wallet to pay, and the wallet decides.

The wallet speaks MCP, the protocol AI agents already use for tools, so any agent that can call a tool can pay. It pays through the owner's own Lightning node: today XBT Lightning invoices (BOLT 11) and offers (BOLT 12), and tickets once they are built in. Payments above the owner's threshold wait for the owner's signature from another device. The policy itself is signed by the owner, so the agent cannot rewrite it.

For tickets the wallet gains a ticket book. The secrets are made inside the wallet's signer, and the agent never sees one before it is spent. Each ticket is counted twice: against a cap on what may be locked when it is locked, and against the budgets when its secret leaves. A released secret is written down before it leaves the signer, so a crash cannot under-count what was spent. Routing fees are counted as spending. All of this is designed, not built: the wallet's Lightning payer today allows one payment in flight at a time, and tickets need several.

The scope of the wallet's claim is narrow. It covers an agent whose only way to the wallet is the MCP tools. Software that can open the wallet's signer socket directly is outside it, so the socket must stay out of the agent's reach. The policy binds only what goes through the wallet: anyone who holds a broader credential for the Lightning node can spend past it. The policy also limits how much an allowed seller can be paid, not what else that seller does. And the outside review found faults in this very part, the wallet's budget accounting; see Status for where that stands. One rule that came out of those fixes carries over to tickets unchanged: the wallet books what it released, never a number the seller sends.

12Built and not built

Built
The agent wallet and its policy engine: budgets, per-seller caps, approvals and the log. The wallet's Lightning payer, which pays BOLT 11 invoices and BOLT 12 offers through the owner's node. The HTTP 402 wire and signed receipts. Fair exchange for fixed content. Pay with work, where a miner pays with pool shares instead of coins. All of it is in the public tree and runs on test networks only.
Lab-tested
Stream tickets. The lock, release, cancel and failure cases in Numbers were run on three Lightning nodes by scripts. The ticket book in the wallet, the seller's software, the 402 entry for tickets and fair exchange on tickets are not built. Tickets to many sellers at once, and both forms of the lottery, were run the same way, by scripts and prototypes on three and five Lightning nodes. Neither form of the lottery is built, and the win that pays itself will not be built before a cryptographer has reviewed the argument it rests on.
Planned
A deeper upgrade: a stream output inside a Lightning channel. Two direct peers would add one output to their channel, and the payer would pay out of it by signing a rising total for each piece, with no Lightning payment round per piece. It would bring back exact settlement per piece, which tickets give up. It needs a new channel type in the Lightning node software, so it can only be done with the maintainers of the XBT Lightning forks, and we mean to raise it with them. As designed it covers two direct peers only; tickets would still carry a payment across a route. For many sellers and for the lottery, the change that would help most is a different one, point locks; see The costs, stated plainly.

13The earlier design

The first version of Lightning Stream did not carry payments over Lightning. It used its own one-way payment channel on the chain: Jeremy Spilman's channel of 2013, in the CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY form that BIP 65 gives as its example. The buyer locked coins once in an output on the chain, signed a slightly larger running total for every piece, and the seller settled once. To reach many sellers with one channel it added a hub, with each forwarded payment locked by an adaptor signature.

An outside review of the published code (October 2026) found the hub the weakest part. One-way channels with no revocation give a hub nothing to fall back on when a channel closes or is replaced while a payment is in flight, and that is the problem Lightning's commitment protocol was built to solve. The review advised using Lightning for what a network is good at, reaching many sellers, and keeping the direct channel as a fast path. We went further and moved the whole payment path onto Lightning.

The hub is retired
No more work is done on it, and it is being taken out of the build and the packages. Lightning's own routing replaces it. The 59 stated limits that belonged to the hub, to the hub beside a Lightning node and to the routed client go with it.
The on-chain channel is frozen
It stays in the source tree and stays tested. Nothing new is built on it and it is off the main path. Whether to remove it will be decided after tickets run end to end. Its one remaining case is a seller who cannot run a Lightning node.
What the channel did that tickets do not
It settled each piece exactly, it left nothing in flight when a buyer stopped, and its seller needed no Lightning node. Tickets give those three up, as What it gives and what it costs says. What they bring is Lightning's routing in place of a hub, and no transaction on the chain for each buyer and seller.

An earlier draft of this paper had a section, "Beside Lightning", which argued that Lightning could not hold a buyer's coins under a lock for the length of a session. Hold invoices do that, for a ticket at a time. That section no longer describes Lightning Stream.

14Prior art

Nothing in a ticket is a new mechanism. It is the hold invoice Lightning nodes already have, used the way "stuckless payments" were proposed for Lightning in 2019, and bound to HTTP 402 and to an agent's spending policy. The policy wallet is the part we think is most useful.

L402
Lightning Labs' HTTP 402 binding: a macaroon plus a Lightning invoice. Used for metered services it becomes a prepaid balance at the seller. A seller that collects each ticket on arrival, before it is used, is running exactly that in ticket-sized units, and a seller whose node has no hold invoices can do so.
Keysend streaming
Podcast apps stream sats with one spontaneous Lightning payment a minute. That is a payment per piece: it suits a service where a wait of a tenth of a second or more per piece does not matter.
x402
The HTTP payment standard whose shape Lightning Stream uses. The binding to XBT is our own, and the upstream clients do not speak it.
Nostr Wallet Connect
NIP-47 budgets let an app spend from a wallet within limits the owner set. The policy wallet applies the same idea to agents, with an owner-signed policy.
Routstr and Cashu
Pay-per-token inference with ecash. Payments are instant and private, but the mint holds the money. A locked ticket is held by nobody: it sits in Lightning channels until the buyer releases it or it returns.
Ark
One operator serving many recipients off chain, the shape the retired hub had. Users can always exit on chain alone, but must come back before their coins' expiry to refresh them. Lightning Stream now has no operator of its own.

Paying by lottery

Paying small amounts by lottery is an old idea. Ronald Rivest described it in 1997, in "Electronic Lottery Tickets as Micropayments", with a bank that honours the winning tickets. Later systems put a contract on a chain in the bank's place: it holds the payer's deposit and pays a winner without the payer's help. Lightning has neither a bank nor such a contract, and that is why the plain form here leaves the seller exposed by about one win.

In May 2026 Paul Gerhart, Jay Taylor and Sri Aravinda Krishnan Thyagarajan published "Probabilistic Atomic Swaps for Bitcoin and Friends" (arXiv:2605.04975), which builds an exchange that completes with a set probability and enforces itself, and demonstrates it on Lightning. The aim of a win that enforces itself off the chain is theirs first. As we read their paper, each of their rolls is a small paid exchange of its own, where ours keeps one payment locked and lets the roll ride on a request the buyer sends anyway; and theirs comes with a formal treatment, where the check ours depends on is our own argument and has not been reviewed.

Where tickets sit

Tickets sit between a prepaid balance and a payment per piece. A prepaid balance hands the seller the money before the service. A payment per piece hands it over piece by piece and makes each piece wait. A ticket commits the money before the service and hands it over a ticket at a time, inside the request.

16Status and limits

Works
On XBT's regtest: the agent wallet pays Lightning invoices and offers through a Lightning Fork node under the owner's policy. On the earlier channel, also on regtest: open, pay, stream, close and refund, and a Claude agent given only the wallet's tools bought an analytics API, refused a tampered chunk, and the seller received exactly what it charged.
Lab-tested
Stream tickets, by scripts on three Lightning nodes; see Numbers. They are not in the wallet or in any release. The same holds for tickets to many sellers and for both forms of the lottery, which ran on three and five nodes.
Reviewed
There has been no independent audit. Our internal review found three critical and five high-severity issues; we changed the code for each and added tests. An outside review of the published code (October 2026) then found more serious issues: in the agent wallet's budget accounting, the hub, the pay-with-work audit and the HTTP layer. Six rounds of fixes are in the public tree. Four follow-up audits of our own checked them: the first found 14 items (3 high), the second 20 (6 high), the third 23 (none high, 11 medium), the fourth 18 (none high, 9 medium). After the sixth round every finding is fixed or ends as a stated limit, and two items were open; a later run of the failure cases through the release binaries added three. All five are on the frozen channel's path or on a rail of the Python reference wallet that is off by default. The source tree's docs/LIMITS.md lists every limit, the open items and what nobody has reviewed. None of this has been re-reviewed by anyone outside the project, and the review was of the earlier design: nobody outside the project has reviewed tickets. Nobody outside the project has reviewed the lottery either: not the fairness of the roll, and not the argument that the win that pays itself rests on. That form will not be built before a cryptographer has reviewed it. Treat it all as unaudited software.
Retired
The hub is retired. The on-chain channel is frozen; see The earlier design.
Not yet
Tickets in the wallet. The seller's software, and the gateway that puts a price on a service already running. The 402 entry for tickets. The lottery, in either form. A test with the Core Lightning fork. XBT mainnet, deliberately on hold: test networks only. An independent audit. A published specification. Packages for Umbrel and StartOS that run beside a Lightning node.
Trade-offs
Money moves a ticket at a time, and the buyer can lose one ticket. Unused tickets are locked until cancelled or expired. The seller needs a Lightning node with inbound capacity and hold invoices, online while tickets are locked. Routing nodes hold tickets without a fee when they are cancelled. Paid by lottery, income and cost are uneven in the short run, and where the buyer pays after a win the seller goes first by about one win. The Lightning nodes on both sides hold hot keys for their channels. XBT's Lightning network is young and small, so a route may not exist.

In one sentence. Lock a few small Lightning payments, release one with every request, take back the rest: a buyer can pay across the Lightning network for anything that arrives over time, and can lose no more than one ticket.
